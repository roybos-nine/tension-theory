# Tension Theory — Biological System Analysis

## Purpose

Use Tension Theory as a lens for analyzing biological systems — organisms, populations, ecosystems, evolution.

Life is not a special category of matter.
An organism is a persistent form — a stable tension loop that continuously updates its internal state to maintain configuration against environmental gradients.
Evolution is not optimization toward a goal. It is undirected exploration of the space of stable configurations under tension.

---

## Vocabulary

**Organism:** A persistent, self-maintaining form — a subgraph that continuously executes local updates to preserve internal tension balance against external gradients.

**Metabolism:** The continuous update cycle that maintains internal tension below collapse threshold; requires constant energy input (tension redistribution capacity) from the environment.

**Fitness:** The stability of a configuration under a given set of environmental tension gradients. Not an intrinsic property — defined relative to the surrounding graph.

**Gene:** A compressed state template that enables reproduction of a persistent form. Encodes the update rules that produce a stable configuration across generations.

**Mutation:** A random perturbation to a state template — most increase tension and fail; rare ones produce new stable configurations under current environmental gradients.

**Natural selection:** Tension-based pruning of update paths over generational time. Configurations that cannot maintain tension balance are removed; those that can, persist.

**Reproduction:** Copying a stable tension configuration into a new node — with bounded fidelity due to finite memory. Source of variation.

**Development:** The process by which a compressed state template (gene) unfolds into a full persistent form through a sequence of local updates.

**Ecosystem:** A distributed network of interacting persistent forms — each maintaining internal tension balance partly by drawing from and contributing to the states of others.

**Symbiosis:** Two persistent forms maintaining mutually low-tension edges — stable because each reduces the other's update cost.

**Predation / Competition:** High-tension interactions where one form's update increases the tension of another's. Drives co-evolution of update rules.

**Death:** Irreversible collapse of the stable tension configuration — internal gradients exceed the organism's capacity to execute compensating updates.

**Adaptation:** Gradual shift in stable configuration in response to persistent environmental tension gradients, over generational time.

**Niche:** The specific environmental tension profile under which a form's update rules produce stability.

## Analysis Algorithm

### Step 1 — Define the Biological Graph

Identify:

- Organisms / populations / species (nodes)
- Interactions: predation, symbiosis, competition, reproduction (edges)
- Environmental inputs: energy, nutrients, temperature (tension sources from outside the graph)

---

### Step 2 — Identify Memory Constraints

For each level of organization ask:

- What state is stored in genes (intergenerational memory)?
- What state is stored in the organism's body and nervous system (within-lifetime memory)?
- What is the fidelity of reproduction (how much state is preserved vs. perturbed)?

---

### Step 3 — Detect Tension Sources

Search for:

- Resource scarcity (constrained inputs limiting update paths)
- Environmental gradients (persistent external tension the organism must counter)
- Predator-prey tension (one node's update directly increases another's tension)
- Reproductive pressure (configurations compete for the same stable niche)
- Developmental instability (update rules that fail to produce stable forms under current conditions)

---

### Step 4 — Analyze Update Dynamics

Ask:

- What update rules maintain internal homeostasis (tension balance)?
- What is the organism's response to environmental tension spikes?
- How do mutations propagate through the population graph over generations?
- What are the cascade effects of removing or adding a node (species)?

---

### Step 5 — Classify System Dynamics

Classify:

- Stable ecosystem (tension balanced across interactions, low extinction risk)
- Adaptable (high variation, multiple stable niches, tension absorbed locally)
- Fragile (low diversity, high interdependence, single-point collapse risk)
- Near collapse (key nodes under unsustainable tension, cascade extinction risk)
- Transforming (major environmental tension shift driving rapid configuration change)

---

## Output Format

Always produce:

1. Biological graph (organisms/populations, interactions, energy flows)
2. Tension map (resource scarcity, predator-prey gradients, environmental pressure)
3. Memory constraints (genetic fidelity, developmental robustness, adaptation rate)
4. Update dynamics (homeostasis rules, evolutionary pressures, cascade risks)
5. Stability classification
6. Predicted evolution of the system under current tension regime
